About

Research beyond the obvious.

Hackathon ’26 guides students through a structured research journey: defining a problem, developing a research question, evaluating existing work, analysing evidence, refining arguments, and producing a complete final project.

Beginners are welcome. Participants are guided through a progressive research process, with each stage building on the one before.

  • 01

    Question

    Identify meaningful problems and formulate focused research questions.

  • 02

    Investigate

    Use credible sources, evaluate evidence, and examine competing explanations.

  • 03

    Build

    Develop a coherent research project with defensible reasoning, appropriate references, and proportionate conclusions.

Policy

AI Policy: Maximum 30%

Generative AI may contribute no more than 30% of the prose retained in your submitted research.

The limit

  • It concerns AI-generated prose retained in the final work, including lightly edited AI passages.
  • Ordinary spelling, grammar and formatting help is not automatically counted as AI-generated prose.
  • Substantive AI drafting or rewriting counts toward the limit.

Your responsibilities

  • Verify AI-generated claims, quotations, statistics, citations, DOIs and URLs against the original sources.
  • Do not use AI to fabricate evidence, invent citations, conceal plagiarism or misrepresent authorship.
  • You are responsible for everything you submit.
  • Do not conceal AI use.

Disclosure

Include an AI-use declaration with your submission stating the tools used, their purpose, the research stages they assisted, and a good-faith estimate of the retained AI-generated prose.

Example declaration (AI used)

I used [tool name] to help brainstorm search terms (Milestone 1) and to suggest edits to the clarity of two paragraphs in the discussion (Milestone 3). I estimate that about 8% of the retained prose was AI-generated or substantially AI-rewritten. I checked all AI-suggested claims and references against the original sources.

Example declaration (no AI used)

I did not use generative AI in this project.

Disclosure does not excuse exceeding the limit, plagiarism or fabricated citations.

AI detectors

An AI-detector score is not a definitive measure of authorship. A detector result alone cannot establish a violation. Compliance is assessed through review, evidence of authorship where relevant and your documented research process. See Enforcement and Appeals.

Procedure

Enforcement and Appeals

An allegation is not proof, and an AI-detector score alone is not proof.

  1. Grounds for review: suspected breach of the official rules, such as plagiarism, fabrication, false authorship or exceeding the AI limit.
  2. Intake and screening: reports are logged and screened to see whether they raise a rule issue.
  3. Private notice: the affected participant or team is told privately.
  4. Chance to respond: normally 72 hours.
  5. Evidence review: evidence is reviewed and conflicts of interest are managed.
  6. Written decision: explains the rule applied and the evidence considered.
  7. Outcomes: proportionate to evidence and seriousness: no action, a request for clarification, correction, a warning, exclusion of affected material, ineligibility or disqualification.
  8. Appeal: one written appeal within 72 hours of the decision.
  9. Appeal review: preferably by someone other than the original decision-maker.
  10. Confidentiality: cases are handled confidentially, with proportionate records.

Grounds for appeal

  • A material factual error
  • Relevant new evidence
  • A significant procedural error
  • Inconsistent application of the published rules

Send appeals to thespeakersfloors.club@gmail.com.

Review-panel details are not shared with participants.

Late Submission Policy

Standard deadline

11:00 PM AST (UTC+3) on the date of the relevant milestone.

  • Up to two hours late: may be accepted as late and may receive reduced feedback or later review.
  • More than two hours late: marked missed unless an organiser approves an exception in writing.
  • Final submission: same two-hour grace period, but late final work is not guaranteed to be considered for awards or final review.
  • A documented serious emergency or verified technical failure may justify an extension.
  • Forgetting the deadline, ordinary workload, misreading the time zone, or a team member being unavailable does not automatically qualify.
  • Requests are not approved until written approval is received.
  • A missed milestone does not automatically expel a participant. Organisers may allow the participant to continue.

How receipt time is determined

Receipt time is the time recorded by the official submission channel the organisers announce. This website has no upload portal and records no timestamps. Keep proof of when you submitted. Submission platforms will be shared with participants via email and WhatsApp.

Policy

Research Integrity

Your work must show your own understanding of the question and your ability to weigh evidence.

Prohibited

  • Copying writing without attribution, or paraphrasing so closely it misleads
  • Presenting others’ ideas, findings or work as your own
  • Fabricating sources, quotations, data, findings or citations
  • Manipulating findings or misrepresenting evidence
  • False authorship, or reusing another person’s work without permission
  • Claiming research you did not carry out

Citing a source does not make extensive copying acceptable. Quoted text must be marked and referenced, and your own analysis must be clearly separate from other researchers’ work.

Expected

  • Credit all sources in Harvard author–date style
  • Verify sources and acknowledge limitations
  • Respect privacy and obtain appropriate permission before research involving people or sensitive information
  • Follow the AI Policy including the 30% limit and disclosure

Official Submission Rules and Regulations

These rules apply to all Hackathon ’26 participants.

AI Policy · Research Integrity · Teamwork and Authorship · Late Submissions · Enforcement and Appeals · Final Checklist

Dates and deadlines

Event: 11 October–28 November 2026. Submissions are due in four biweekly milestones, each at 11:00 PM AST (UTC+3): Milestone 1: 24 October 2026, Milestone 2: 7 November 2026, Milestone 3: 21 November 2026, Milestone 4: 28 November 2026. You should keep researching throughout; the deadlines set the formal submission schedule.

File requirements

  • Final submissions must be PDF or Microsoft Word DOCX.
  • There is no fixed word-count limit. A submission must still contain enough research, evidence, analysis and explanation to be a complete project.
  • Team file: TeamName_Track.pdf or .docx. Individual file: ParticipantName_Track.pdf or .docx.
  • Replace spaces with underscores. Use the registered name and the official track name, or a clearly recognisable version.

Filename examples

  • Valid (team): Green_Falcons_Technology_and_AI.pdf
  • Valid (individual): Aisha_Khan_Environment_and_Sustainability.docx
  • Invalid: final version 2.pdf (no name or track, has spaces)
  • Invalid: Green Falcons Tech.pdf (spaces)
  • Invalid: Aisha_Khan_Health.pptx (wrong file type)

Correcting a filename: rename the file to match the pattern, check the name matches your registration, and resubmit through the official channel with a short note saying only the filename changed. Resubmission after a deadline is subject to the late policy.

What a complete final document contains

Where appropriate for your discipline:

  • Research title
  • Registered author or team names
  • Selected track
  • Abstract or concise summary
  • Research question and problem statement
  • Background and relevant existing research
  • Methodology or research approach
  • Evidence, sources, data and analysis
  • Limitations and uncertainty
  • Evidence-based conclusion
  • Harvard in-text citations
  • Complete reference list
  • AI-use declaration

Disciplines differ. A legal analysis, a data analysis and a biomedical literature review do not need the same structure. Use the structure that suits your question and explain your approach.

Harvard author–date referencing (mandatory)

Use Harvard author–date referencing consistently. Disciplines and institutions vary in small details, so this is the house guide for Hackathon ’26. The patterns below are formats, not real references.

SourcePattern
ParaphraseSmith (2022) argues that … or … (Smith, 2022). Rewrite fully in your own words and still cite.
Direct quotation'Quoted words' (Smith, 2022, p. 14). Use rarely and keep it short.
Journal articleSurname, A.B. (Year) 'Title of article', Journal Name, volume(issue), pages. doi or URL.
BookSurname, A.B. (Year) Title of book. Edition. Place: Publisher.
Organisational reportOrganisation (Year) Title of report. Place: Publisher. Available at: URL (Accessed: day month year).
WebpageOrganisation or Author (Year) Title of page. Available at: URL (Accessed: day month year).
DatasetCreator (Year) Title of dataset [Dataset]. Version. Publisher. Available at: URL (Accessed: day month year).
Figure or tableFigure 1: Title. Source: Surname (Year, p. x). Or, for your own: Figure 2: Title. Source: author's analysis of Creator (Year).
Case, statute, treatyCase: Party v Party [Year] Court Report. Statute: Title of Act Year (Jurisdiction). Treaty: Title of treaty (Year) followed by the treaty series reference. Name the jurisdiction and keep one consistent format.
Several authorsTwo authors: (Smith and Jones, 2021). Three or more: (Smith et al., 2021) in text, with the authors listed in the reference list.
No individual authorUse the organisation as author: (World Health Organization, 2023). If none exists, use the title in quotation marks and year. Do not use 'Anon.' unless the source says so.

Checking your references

  • Confirm the source exists by finding it through the publisher, a library or a database.
  • Open it and confirm it supports the claim you attach to it.
  • Check author, year, title, publisher and link against the source itself.
  • Check every in-text citation appears in the reference list and the reverse.
  • Never cite a source you have not seen, and never cite an AI tool in place of the original source.

For further help, consult a library Harvard guide from your school or university, or the book Cite Them Right by Pears and Shields (paywalled).

Research integrity

These are prohibited: plagiarism, unattributed copying, misleading paraphrasing, fabricated sources, fabricated data, fake quotations, manipulated findings, false authorship and deliberate misrepresentation. Citing a source does not make extensive copying acceptable. Separate your own analysis from other researchers’ work. See Research Integrity and the AI Policy.

Policy

Teamwork and Authorship

  • The official registration record determines team membership.
  • Only registered participants may be listed as authors, unless an organiser approves a change in writing.
  • Listed authors must make meaningful contributions.
  • Prohibited: nominal authorship, excluding genuine contributors, falsely claiming another person’s contribution, and submitting the same project under multiple teams.

Working as a team

  • Agree author order early and write it down.
  • Keep a simple record of who did what.
  • Settle disagreements by discussing them and noting the outcome.

Authorship disputes

  1. Try to resolve it within the team and document the attempt.
  2. If unresolved, contact the organisers privately at thespeakersfloors.club@gmail.com.
  3. Provide relevant contribution records and explanations.
  4. Affected people are given a chance to respond.
  5. The decision is recorded and communicated privately.

Maximum team size: 4 members.

This is a research track, not a pre-assigned project. Choose your own focused question and methods that fit it. No project requires experiments, surveys or coding.

Example research question

Illustrative example only

How closely do publicly reported air-quality measurements from different monitoring networks agree for the same city?

You may develop your own question.

How to begin

Begin with a question a public dataset could answer, then read the dataset's documentation first.

How to evaluate sources

Check who collected the data, when, how, what is missing and whether the sample represents your population.

Common mistakes

  • Analysing data without checking provenance
  • Inferring cause from correlation
  • Presenting a chart without units or source

Research ethics and limitations

Check data provenance, privacy, bias and representativeness. Keep raw data, analysis, interpretation and conclusions separate.

Topic & Research Proposal

Milestone 1 of 4. This shows your place in the guide only. It does not record any submission.

Deadline

24 October 2026, 11:00 PM AST (UTC+3)

Up to two hours late may be accepted as late. After that it is marked missed unless an organiser approves an exception in writing. See the late policy.

Purpose of this milestone

Choose a track, define a focused research question, and plan how you will investigate it. A clear question and a sound plan keep every later step on track.

You should keep researching throughout the event. The biweekly deadlines set the formal submission schedule, not the pace of your work.

What to submit

Required submission

  • Selected track
  • Proposed research title
  • Focused research question
  • Problem explanation
  • Research objectives
  • Scope of the research
  • Significance of the research
  • Preliminary methodology
  • Initial credible sources

How to do it

  1. Pick a track and a broad subject you can name in a few words, then run preliminary searches to see what is already studied.
  2. Narrow it by population, place, time period or aspect until you could answer it with sources you can reach.
  3. Write one focused question. Avoid yes/no wording and loaded language. Write a problem explanation (about 150 words): the issue, the evidence gap and why it matters.
  4. Write 2–4 objectives (what you will do to answer the question), a scope (what is in and out) and a significance statement (who benefits from the answer).
  5. Choose a preliminary method that fits the question (for example a source review, public-data analysis, case study or legal analysis) and justify it. Do not claim interviews, surveys or experiments have happened if they have not. Note any ethical issues.
  6. List initial credible sources and check each one exists and supports what you plan to use it for.

Is AI bad?Too broad and biasedWhat do published audits report about accuracy differences across demographic groups in facial-analysis systems?
What is climate change?Descriptive and too broadHow do published life-cycle studies compare lifetime emissions of electric and petrol cars?
Should we ban X?Opinion, assumes a conclusionHow have jurisdictions A and B regulated X, and what outcomes do evaluations report?
Does one vitamin cure disease Y?Assumes cause and can be unanswerableWhat does systematic-review evidence say about the vitamin and outcome Y?

Your question is narrow and answerable, your plan fits it, and your sources are credible.

  • Assuming the answer in the question
  • Scope too large to finish in the time
  • Objectives that only repeat the question

Literature Review & Evidence

Milestone 2 of 4. This shows your place in the guide only. It does not record any submission.

Deadline

7 November 2026, 11:00 PM AST (UTC+3)

Up to two hours late may be accepted as late. After that it is marked missed unless an organiser approves an exception in writing. See the late policy.

Purpose of this milestone

Study existing research, then collect and begin analysing your own evidence. You cannot add to a field you have not read, and you need evidence to answer your question.

You should keep researching throughout the event. The biweekly deadlines set the formal submission schedule, not the pace of your work.

What to submit

Required submission

  • Structured literature or source review
  • Annotated credible sources
  • Identified research gaps or limitations
  • Evidence collected
  • Preliminary analysis

How to do it

  1. Search systematically and record your search terms and databases. Screen sources by relevance, date, credibility and type (primary or secondary, peer reviewed or not).
  2. Annotate each source: what it contributes, its credibility or limits, and how it relates to your question.
  3. Group sources by theme and compare their findings. Do not just summarise one source after another.
  4. Identify disagreements, limitations and possible gaps. Do not claim no research exists just because a quick search found none.
  5. Collect your evidence and record its source, date and context. Organise it by theme or sub-question.
  6. Begin analysing it in a way that fits your question. Quantitative work: check provenance, units and missing values, use descriptive statistics and charts, and avoid causal claims. Legal and policy work: name the jurisdiction, primary authorities, implementation and competing interpretations. Other tracks: use methods that fit, such as thematic analysis of documents or case comparison.

What a strong submission shows

You compare sources by theme, judge their credibility, and collect evidence you can trace to its source.

Common mistakes

  • A list of summaries instead of a synthesis
  • Treating all sources as equally credible
  • Mixing facts with your interpretation
  • Ignoring contradictory evidence

What to submit

How to do it
